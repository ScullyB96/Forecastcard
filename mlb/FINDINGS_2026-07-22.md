# Session Findings — 2026-07-22

A second external review of `MODEL_DOCUMENTATION.md` argued the highest-value work left wasn't another candidate signal, but structural gaps in the model architecture itself — plus a methodology problem (small backtest sample) that was leaving several earlier results unresolved. This document summarizes what was checked, what was fixed, and what the numbers actually say.

## TL;DR

- **The model's real accuracy is lower than earlier headline numbers suggested.** Scaling the backtest from ~600 games to the full 7,237-game 2023–2025 dataset dropped straight-up accuracy from the previously-cited 58–60.5% down to **56.3%**. The smaller samples were likely noise-inflated, not just imprecise.
- **Two real architectural bugs were found and fixed**: the model had no home-field advantage anywhere, and player rate estimates were double-counting park effects. Both are now fixed. Their exact effect size is still unresolved at typical sample sizes (see below), but they're correctness fixes, not hypotheses — kept regardless.
- **One brand-new signal is a confirmed, real win**: a pitcher's groundball/flyball tendency measurably predicts their home-run-allowed rate, beyond what their own uneven track record shows. This is the first pitcher-side signal to work in this project after six failed attempts.
- **One candidate fix (rookie priors) was tested and cleanly rejected.** Real effect at the player level, no measurable benefit to the model as a whole.
- **Two previously-"kept" signals (bat speed, pulled-air rate) remain unresolved**, not proven either way — including a self-caught mistake in how they were re-tested, explained below.

---

## 1. Two free diagnostics, run before building anything

Two measurements against the existing backtest data, at zero build cost:

- **Home win-rate gap**: the simulator predicted home teams win 50.6% of the time; real MLB history says 52.3%. A ~1.7 percentage point gap.
- **Total-runs dispersion**: real game totals are more spread out than the simulator's own uncertainty band suggests — extreme high/low-scoring games happen about 2.5x more often in reality than the model's Monte Carlo trials predict. **This one is still unresolved — flagged, not fixed, this round.**

Both confirmed real, moderate problems worth investigating further.

## 2. Home-field advantage — built, kept, effect size still unclear

The model had no mechanism for home teams having any inherent edge — every contextual factor (park, weather, platoon splits, etc.) applied identically to both teams. Real MLB home teams win roughly 52.5–54% of games; the model was blind to this.

**Root cause of why it was missing**: the model already computes each team's park-specific scoring boost/penalty (e.g., Coors Field inflates offense), but that calculation includes a normalization step that — as a side effect — also cancels out any *league-wide* home advantage baked equally into every team's data. Fixed by adding a separate, pooled-across-all-teams home/road ratio that isn't subject to that cancellation.

**Result**: verified in isolation that the mechanism works correctly (produces a realistic ~0.2–0.3 extra runs per game for the home team). But a proper paired A/B test on the existing ~600-game sample couldn't statistically distinguish "with fix" from "without" — the sample is just too small to detect an effect this size. **Kept anyway**, because this is fixing a real, confirmed gap, not chasing an unproven hypothesis — the same logic used earlier for a known rules-correctness fix (extra-innings ghost runner) that also didn't move the needle in testing but is unambiguously correct to include.

## 3. Park-adjusted player rates — built, kept, same caveat as above

Player performance history was being used raw — a hitter's rates were already inflated or suppressed by whatever ballparks they happened to play in, and then the model applied its park adjustment *on top of that*, double-counting the effect. Concentrated in extreme parks (Coors Field, pre-humidor Chase Field).

Fixed by neutralizing each historical plate appearance for that specific game's park effect before it feeds into a player's rate estimate — confirmed directionally correct on real data (Rockies hitters' park-neutral home run rate came out lower than their raw rate, as expected; Mariners hitters' came out higher).

Same caveat as home-field advantage: correct fix, confirmed mechanism, but not statistically distinguishable from "no fix" at the smaller sample size. Kept regardless.

## 4. Scaling the backtest — the biggest single finding this session

The existing test protocol used ~600–1,000 games sampled from 2024–2025. All three MLB seasons since the 2023 rules changes (pitch clock, shift ban, bigger bases, extra-innings ghost runner) are actually available — 7,277 real games total. Rebuilt the backtest to use all of them, at a reduced per-game simulation count to keep runtime reasonable (the loss in per-game precision is a much smaller cost than the gain from testing 12x more games).

**The new, much more reliable baseline: 56.3% straight-up accuracy, down from the 58–60.5% cited earlier this session.**

This doesn't mean the model got worse — the model didn't change. It means the earlier, smaller-sample numbers were probably never as good as they looked; they were partly good luck in which games happened to get sampled. This is exactly the kind of illusion a small test sample can create, and it's a big part of why the next two findings needed re-checking.

## 5. Pitcher groundball/flyball rate → home-run-allowed — a real, new win

The one prior signal type that had never worked in this project was anything on the pitcher side — five separate attempts (pitch-mix quality, swing-and-miss rate, etc.) all failed to hold up. The reasoning here was different: pitchers are well-established (in outside research) to have real, fast-stabilizing control over whether contact results in a groundball or a flyball — even though they don't control what happens once that ball is in play. Since home-run-allowed rate is exactly the kind of outcome that's slow and noisy to estimate directly, a pitcher's groundball/flyball tendency should sharpen that estimate.

Checked directly against real data first: pitchers with a heavier groundball tendency measurably allow fewer future home runs than their own raw track record alone would suggest, and it roughly **doubles** the predictive power of the home-run-allowed estimate. This was the single largest improvement-in-predictive-power found for any one signal this entire project.

Built and tested on the full 7,237-game protocol: **a confirmed, statistically real improvement in straight-up accuracy (56.3% → 57.9%)**. This is now live in every part of the pipeline, including the live daily prediction generator.

## 6. Rookie/no-history player projections — tested, cleanly rejected

Players with no MLB track record were being projected at the league-average rate. Real rookies perform below average — that's close to the definition of "replacement level." Built a fix using the actual historical performance of debut-season players instead of the league average, and confirmed it behaves sensibly (real rookies strike out ~2-3 percentage points more than average, walk and homer somewhat less).

Tested on the full 7,237-game protocol: **no measurable benefit to the model overall.** The result was decisively flat, not just unclear — a tight, confident "this doesn't help," not "we can't tell." The code is built and available but switched off; it's not deployed.

## 7. Bat speed and pulled-air rate — still unresolved, and a mistake caught along the way

Two signals from earlier in the project (a batter's bat speed improving contact-quality estimates, and a batter's tendency to pull fly balls improving home-run-share estimates) had been "kept" originally, then found statistically inconclusive on a mid-size retest. With the new 7,277-game protocol built, it seemed like the right moment to settle it for good.

**First attempt, and a real mistake**: comparing the "what if these signals were off" test against the wrong reference point (one that predated a different change made earlier the same day) made both signals look like they were making things measurably *worse* — enough that reverting them looked justified. Before acting on that, the result was double-checked because it looked too dramatic to trust outright, and reversing two previously-celebrated wins is a big enough decision to deserve a second look. The dramatic result turned out to be a comparison error, not a real finding — it had picked up an unrelated change instead of these two signals' own effect.

**Corrected test, against the right reference point**: both signals are, once again, **statistically inconclusive** — neither helping nor hurting in a way the data can distinguish from chance, now with a meaningfully tighter margin of error than before. Nothing was changed in the codebase; both signals remain in place, still unproven either way.

## What's genuinely settled vs. still open

**Settled, confirmed, and acted on:**
- Home win-rate gap and total-runs dispersion — real, confirmed problems (dispersion still unfixed)
- Home-field advantage — real gap, fixed, kept
- Park-double-counting — real bug, fixed, kept
- Backtest scale — the 7,237-game protocol is now the standing reference, replacing every earlier smaller-sample number
- Pitcher groundball/flyball signal — real, confirmed, deployed
- Rookie-prior fix — real player-level effect, no model-level benefit, correctly left switched off

**Still open:**
- Total-runs dispersion — diagnosed, no fix attempted yet
- Whether home-field-advantage/park fixes move the needle at the new, larger sample size — not yet re-tested there
- Bat speed and pulled-air rate — still genuinely unresolved
- Three smaller items flagged but not investigated: base-stealing modeling ignoring the opposing catcher/pitcher, the bullpen model ignoring a reliever's back-to-back-day performance (not just rest), and an aging-curve constant that's roughly a decade out of date versus current research
